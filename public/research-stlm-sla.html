Small models that actually reason

Meaning axes, then an explicit memory — raced against a matched Transformer on one consumer GPU.

Small-scale programme · STLM · Mini-SLA · The Belna team

A 20-million-parameter Transformer can finish a sentence. It cannot tell you that hot is the opposite of cold, or that not open means closed. Those failures are not a data problem. They are a representation problem.

We ran two tiny architectures against a matched baseline, on a single consumer GPU, in minutes. First we pin words to meaning. Then we put negation in the architecture so the model does not need a hint at inference.

STLM understands opposites and still needs a nudge. Mini-SLA goes 35/35 on negation with zero help.

Model card 01

STLM

Semantic Topology Language Model. Same Transformer core as the baseline, plus a topology classifier, a coordinate regressor, and one reusable axis embedding per meaning. Opposites live on opposite ends of an axis. The baseline files “hot” and “cold” as similar because they appear in similar sentences.

Geometry for meaning, help still needed

STLM’s vector space understands opposites. Negation still needs the constraint bias at inference (0/35 plain). Grammar stays with the Transformer. That is the gap the next model is for.

Model card 02

Mini-SLA

Geometry can hold meaning and still need a hint. Mini-SLA puts negation in the architecture instead. One Transformer layer, applied four times, wrapped around an explicit key-value memory with ten entity slots. A symbolic router flips retrieval when not is in play. Memory is a database. Compute is a loop. Negation is a switch — which is why SLA needs no constraint injection.

Negation is solvable at tiny scale

SLA goes 35/35 with no inference help. Memory networks are harder to optimize, and grammar still sits with the Transformer. An idea that works at 11M earns its GPU budget at 100M.

  • −Grammar 41–40 / 50 vs 46 / 50. Syntax dips when you structure for meaning.
